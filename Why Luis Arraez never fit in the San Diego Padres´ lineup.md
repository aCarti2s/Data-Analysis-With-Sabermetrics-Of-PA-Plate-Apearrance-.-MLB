When the San Diego Padres signed Luis Arraez as free agent to the San Francisco Giants this past offseason, the reaction was predictably split. One side saw a three-time batting champion being dumped at a discount. The other side pointed to his .292 average in 2025 and called it addition by subtraction. Both sides missed the point entirely.

This isn’t a story about whether Arraez is good or bad. He’s good — historically good at one very specific thing. This is a story about fit. About how a front office can acquire a genuinely elite player and still make a mistake, because the player’s skillset and the team’s environment were working against each other from day one. And it’s about how San Francisco identified all of that, waited, and bought low on a player whose underlying skills never actually went anywhere.

Let’s go through the data.

PLOT C — BA vs. xBA (2022–2026): The Skill That Statcast Doesn’t Fully See

The first thing you need to understand about Luis Arraez is that the standard expected metrics were never built for him.

Statcast calculates xBA — expected batting average — using exit velocity and launch angle. The model looks at every batted ball, compares it to the historical outcomes of similar contact, and spits out what a player should be hitting given the quality of contact he’s making. It’s a useful tool for identifying hitters who are getting lucky on weak contact or unlucky on hard contact. For most hitters, BA and xBA converge over a large enough sample.

Arraez breaks that model every single year:

- 2022: BA .316, xBA .289 (+.027)
- 2023: BA .354, xBA .327 (+.027)
- 2024: BA .314, xBA .310 (+.004)
- 2025: BA .292, xBA .287 (+.005)
- 2026: BA .315, xBA .300 (+.013, partial sample)

One year of outperforming xBA is noise. Two years is interesting. Five consecutive years is a skill, and a rare one. What Statcast’s model doesn’t capture well is directional precision — the ability to consistently place the ball in specific spots on the field, regardless of how hard it’s hit. Arraez doesn’t beat the shift by accident. He hits the ball where fielders aren’t, on purpose, repeatedly.

The concerning part of this chart isn’t the gap — it’s what happened to it in 2024 and 2025. That +.027 he posted in back-to-back years shrank to +.004 and +.005. For the first time in his career, Arraez was hitting almost exactly what his contact quality predicted. His placement skill didn’t disappear, but something clearly disrupted it. Whether that was mechanical, mental, or environmental is something we’ll get to. What matters here is that the gap is already recovering in 2026 with San Francisco — back up to +.013 in a partial sample.

PLOT D — BABIP vs. Hard Hit% (2022–2026): The Paradox That Defines His Value

This is the plot that should settle every argument about whether Arraez’s production is real or whether he’s just been getting lucky on weak contact.

The conventional wisdom in modern baseball analysis is straightforward: hard contact drives BABIP. If you’re hitting the ball at 95+ mph consistently, more of those balls find gaps, beat infielders, and fall in. The correlation between Hard Hit% and BABIP across the league is real and well-documented. When a player posts a high BABIP on soft contact, the standard assumption is that it will regress.

Arraez has been defying that assumption for five years:

- 2022: BABIP .331, Hard Hit% 30.2%
- 2023: BABIP .362, Hard Hit% 25.7%
- 2024: BABIP .324, Hard Hit% 23.7%
- 2025: BABIP .289, Hard Hit% 16.8%
- 2026: BABIP .330, Hard Hit% 17.8%

Look at 2023. He posted a .362 BABIP — one of the highest marks in baseball that year — while hitting the ball hard only 25.7% of the time. The league average Hard Hit% sits around 38-42%. He was operating at roughly two-thirds of league average in contact quality and still posting an elite BABIP. That’s not luck. That’s a skill the standard model undervalues.

Now look at the league average Hard Hit% hovering around 39-40% throughout this entire period. Arraez never came close to it. Not once. His entire offensive value is built on something completely different from what the rest of the league is doing. His BABIP doesn’t live in the same ecosystem as everyone else’s.

The 2025 collapse is the most important data point here. His Hard Hit% cratered to 16.8% — the lowest of any qualified hitter in MLB that season — and his BABIP finally fell below .290 for the first time in his career. But here’s the critical detail: his BABIP at .289 was still above the league average BABIP. Even in his worst season, operating with the softest contact of any regular hitter in baseball, he was still getting on base at an above-average rate on balls in play.

And then 2026 happens. Same Hard Hit% — 17.8%, basically identical to his 2025 floor. But his BABIP jumps back to .330. He didn’t suddenly start hitting the ball harder. He recovered his placement precision. That’s the whole story.

PLOT A — Exit Velocity Distribution (2022–2026): A Profile Built for Consistency, Not Power

The violin plot gives you the full picture of what kind of hitter Arraez actually is, year by year.

Each violin represents the complete distribution of every batted ball he put in play during that season. The width of the violin at any given velocity tells you how many balls he hit at that speed. The colored dots show individual batted balls broken into three zones: soft contact (60-80 mph, in blue), medium contact (80-95 mph, in orange), and hard contact (95+ mph, in red). The black line connecting the mean exit velocities across seasons tells you the trend.

A few things jump out immediately.

First, the shape of his violins is completely consistent across all five years. They’re wide in the orange zone (80-95 mph), narrow at the top (95+), and have a longer tail at the bottom than you’d see from a power hitter. This is not a player whose profile is changing. He has always been a medium-contact hitter with exceptional placement, and his violin plots look essentially the same in 2022 as they do in 2026.

Second, the mean exit velocity line drops steadily from around 89 mph in 2022 to roughly 87 mph in 2025, before stabilizing in 2026. That’s not a dramatic fall, but for a player who was already operating below the league average in contact quality, every mile per hour matters. When your entire offensive game is built on placement rather than power, the margin for error is smaller than it is for a slugger who can compensate with raw exit velocity.

Third — and this is what connects Plot A to Plot D — look at the 2025 violin specifically. The blue zone at the bottom is noticeably wider than in previous years. More soft contact, more balls in the 60-75 mph range. When those balls aren’t perfectly placed, they’re easy outs. That’s the mechanical explanation for what you saw in the BABIP chart.

The 2026 violin already looks more like 2023 and 2024 than it does like 2025. The distribution is tightening back into his historical range.

PLOT B — Spray Chart (2022–2026): Where the Hits Actually Live

The spray chart with density overlay makes the placement argument visual in a way that the numbers alone can’t.

The dark purple and orange zones — where the density is highest — tell you exactly where Arraez concentrates his contact. The heat map is centered in two main areas: up the middle into center field, and to the left-center gap. For a left-handed hitter, that’s the opposite field and center — not pull-side power, not elevated fly balls to right field, but line drives and sharp grounders to the largest part of the field.

This is intentional. Arraez has been one of the most disciplined opposite-field hitters in baseball for years. His swing is designed to make contact and redirect the ball, not to generate power through the pull side. The spray chart makes it immediately obvious why his launch angle is consistently low (around 11-13 degrees) and why his Hard Hit% is irrelevant to evaluating his actual offensive contribution.

The gaps in the density map — particularly in right-center and deep left — are just as telling as the concentrated zones. He’s not trying to hit the ball there, and he doesn’t.

PLOT E — Contact Profile by Park (Petco 2025 vs. Oracle 2026): The Environmental Argument

This is where the trade makes complete sense from San Francisco’s perspective.

Side by side, you have the same hitter putting the ball in essentially the same places. The spray pattern in Oracle Park in 2026 and Petco Park in 2025 is nearly identical — that hot zone up the middle and to left-center is present in both charts. Arraez didn’t change his approach between the two environments.

What changed is what those batted balls ran into.

Petco Park’s dimensions work against exactly the kind of contact Arraez produces. Center field at Petco extends to 396 feet, and the power alleys are deep. The balls that Arraez hits into left-center and up the middle — those medium-exit-velocity line drives that make up the core of his offense — have more ground to cover before they reach the wall. Outfielders have more time to get to them. What’s a single in a smaller park becomes a routine out at Petco.

Oracle Park is a different environment. The dimensions favor contact hitters who work to the opposite field and up the middle. The park suppresses home runs, which is why power hitters have always struggled there, but it doesn’t hurt a hitter like Arraez at all. His contact profile is actually well-suited to what Oracle rewards.

The density maps show this too. In Oracle, the hot zone is more concentrated — his balls in play are clustering in the areas where the park gives him the best chance. In Petco, the density spreads out more, which reflects the larger fair territory eating up what would otherwise be hits.

San Francisco didn’t just acquire a batting average guy. They acquired a hitter whose contact profile maps almost perfectly onto their home park, at a price that reflected two years of underperformance in an environment that was working against him the entire time.

PLOT F — SDP 2025 Offensive Roles (ISO vs. Hard Hit%): He Was Never the Right Fit

The scatter plot of the 2025 Padres lineup is the most damning piece of evidence that this was a roster construction problem, not a player decline.

Every dot represents a Padres hitter with meaningful playing time in 2025. The x-axis is Hard Hit%, the y-axis is ISO (Isolated Power, which measures extra-base hit production). The size of each dot represents home run total. Arraez is in red.

He’s completely alone in the bottom-left corner.

The Padres built their offense around Tatis Jr., Machado, Laureano, and Merrill — all of whom cluster in the upper-right quadrant, combining high Hard Hit% with above-average ISO. That’s the identity of the lineup: impact contact, power production, extra-base threats up and down the order. The dashed lines show the group averages, and the power core label describes where most of the lineup lives.

Arraez is not just below average on both metrics — he’s in a completely different category. His Hard Hit% of 16.8% sits more than 20 percentage points below the lineup average. His ISO of .100 is the lowest on the roster. In a lineup built around generating power and hard contact, he was structurally incompatible.

This isn’t a criticism of Arraez. O’Hearn and Iglesias are also below the group averages, but even they have more Hard Hit% than Arraez. The point is that the Padres assembled a roster with a specific offensive identity, and then had a hitter whose entire value comes from a completely different skill set slotted into it. The lineup protection wasn’t there, the park wasn’t right, and the team’s offensive philosophy wasn’t built around what he does well.

In that context, his .292 average and down BABIP in 2025 make complete sense. He wasn’t declining. He was the wrong player in the wrong place.

----

THE CONCLUSION

Luis Arraez is one of the most unique hitters in baseball. He operates on a margin that almost no other player in the modern game works within — low exit velocity, elite placement, consistent BABIP that defies what the models predict. That skill is real, it’s repeatable, and five years of data prove it.

The Padres had him for parts of two seasons in an environment that suppressed exactly what makes him valuable. His Hard Hit% collapsed, his placement precision eroded, and his BABIP fell for the first time in his career. From the outside it looked like a player in decline. From the inside it was a bad fit that gradually wore him down.

San Francisco saw past the surface numbers. They identified a player whose contact profile maps onto Oracle Park, whose underlying skill of directional placement is historically durable, and who was available at a price reflecting two years of underperformance in the wrong context.

The 2026 numbers — .315 BA, .330 BABIP, positive xBA gap already recovering — suggest they were right.
